Local Restaurateur Dies in Record Heat After
Elaborate Insurance Fraud Scheme Unravels

Harold Haldeman, 48, owner of the La Cienega eatery "Haldeman's," was discovered dead in the rear courtyard of the Ranchero Motel — trapped in freshly-laid tar while awaiting a call that was meant to confirm his alibi.

By Mary K. Calloway, Crime Correspondent  |  Published August 14, 1990, 6:00 AM  |  Reporting contributed by the Palm Springs Police Dept.

Photo illustration: The Ranchero Motel courtyard where Haldeman's body was discovered Sunday evening. The freshly-applied tar, still liquid in the extreme heat, proved fatal. — PST Staff

A Los Angeles restaurant owner who traveled to Palm Springs to establish a false alibi while his own business burned died Sunday in what authorities are calling one of the most self-defeating crimes in the history of the Coachella Valley. Harold Haldeman had constructed what he believed to be a foolproof insurance fraud scheme — only for every element of the plan to betray him at the worst possible moment.

According to the Palm Springs Police Department, Haldeman drove to the remote Ranchero Motel on Friday night, intending to be far from Los Angeles when his chef, Jose, accidentally ignited gas-filled pipes that Haldeman himself had tampered with. The explosion, which never came — Jose called in sick that day — would have destroyed the restaurant and allowed Haldeman to collect an insurance payout on a business he had falsely portrayed as highly profitable.

"He was meticulous about the guest book. Pressed the pen real hard, made sure the date was clear. I thought it was odd." — Ricky, manager of the Ranchero Motel

The scheme began unraveling when Haldeman discovered that the motel's individual rooms had no telephone — a critical flaw in a plan whose entire success depended on receiving a phone call from his business manager, Larry Hyatt, as proof of his whereabouts. The only available phone was located across a large rear courtyard.

What Haldeman did not know was that Mexican workmen had finished laying fresh tar in that very courtyard the previous day. Under the August sun — temperatures exceeded 115°F that afternoon — the tar had failed to harden and returned to a near-liquid state, concealed under a thin layer of sand. When the telephone rang at 3:00 PM, Haldeman ran barefoot across the courtyard and became fatally trapped.

Investigators note the cruel irony: Hyatt called four times in total. On the first three calls, Haldeman was still alive but unable to reach the phone. By the time the fourth ring faded, he had succumbed to the extreme heat and his injuries. The restaurant, meanwhile, remained standing — Jose's illness having inadvertently prevented the explosion.

Haldeman's plan involved falsified financial records maintained by Hyatt, who, investigators say, had no knowledge of the fraud or arson plot. Hyatt "never suspected a thing," according to sources close to the investigation, having based his accounts entirely on false data provided by Haldeman.

Characters in This Case

ProtagonistHarold Haldeman

A 48-year-old Los Angeles restaurateur whose financial desperation led him to devise an insurance fraud and arson scheme. Once heavy-set, he has grown visibly thin from stress. Described as calculating but ultimately defeated by his own plan. Divorced, in debt, and gambling again at the time of his death.

Unwitting AccompliceLarry Hyatt

Haldeman's loyal, trusting business manager for two years. Hyatt unknowingly maintained fraudulent accounts, believing the figures he entered were real. He had no knowledge of the arson plot and spent Sunday afternoon trying to reach Haldeman by phone — an act that marks the story's grim finale.

Intended VictimJose

The restaurant's skilled head chef. Haldeman cynically calculated that Jose would be "blown to pieces" when he lit the sabotaged gas burners. Jose unknowingly saved his own life by calling in sick on the day of the planned explosion.

WitnessRicky — Motel Manager

The rough-edged, bearded manager of the Ranchero Motel who stays in Palm Springs through August to rebuild the property each summer. He provides Haldeman with cold beer and casual conversation, oblivious to the role his motel will play in the man's death.

Key Locations

  • Haldeman's Restaurant — La Cienega & Santa Monica Blvd., Los Angeles. A barbecue restaurant in rapid decline.
  • Haldeman's Apartment — Los Angeles. Where he drinks and reviews his plan the night before leaving.
  • Ranchero Motel — Rancho Mirage, Palm Springs. An old, neglected wooden motel serving as Haldeman's alibi base — and the scene of his death.
  • The Rear Courtyard — Behind the motel. Freshly tarred, dangerously hot, and bearing the single payphone Haldeman needed to answer. The fatal trap.

Inside the Haldeman Affair: 7 Questions Answered

Click to reveal answers
Why was Haldeman planning to burn down his own restaurant?+
Haldeman was facing financial ruin. The restaurant was losing money due to a shift in fashionable clientele to downtown locations, and he was also burdened by his divorce from Mona, his apartment costs, and his gambling debts. With no way out, he planned to commit insurance fraud: burn the restaurant and collect the payout.
What role did Larry Hyatt play — and was he guilty?+
Hyatt was Haldeman's business manager and an unwitting accomplice. He produced completely falsified financial accounts showing the restaurant as highly profitable, but he did so believing the figures were real. Haldeman had fed him false data and then secretly altered Hyatt's computer entries himself. Hyatt "never suspected a thing."
How did Haldeman sabotage the restaurant to cause the fire?+
Before leaving for Palm Springs on Friday, Haldeman partially blocked the gas taps on the kitchen burners. When turned off at night, two taps would remain half-open, slowly filling the pipes beneath the barbecue with gas. When Jose came in on Saturday afternoon and lit the burners, the accumulated gas would cause an explosion — with Jose right beside it.
Why did Haldeman need a phone call from Hyatt?+
The phone call was meant to confirm Haldeman's alibi. If Hyatt could testify he had called Haldeman at the Palm Springs motel at the moment the restaurant caught fire, it would prove Haldeman was miles away and could not have set the fire himself. Without that call being received, his alibi had no witness to confirm it.
Why did Haldeman's plan ultimately fail on both sides?+
The plan collapsed from two directions simultaneously. On the Los Angeles end, Jose called in sick, so no one lit the gas burners — the explosion and fire never happened. On the Palm Springs end, Haldeman's motel room had no telephone, forcing him to run across a freshly-tarred courtyard to reach the only available phone, where he became fatally trapped in the liquid tar.
What is the significance of the extreme heat in the story?+
The heat serves as the story's true antagonist. Haldeman chose Palm Springs in August precisely because almost no one goes there — it gave him a perfect, isolated alibi. But the same ferocious heat that emptied the town also prevented the newly-applied tar from hardening, turning the courtyard into a deadly trap. The very detail that seemed to make the plan safer became the instrument of his death.
What is the main moral or theme of "Cooking the Books"?+
The story is a sharp parable about hubris and greed. Haldeman believed his plan was so carefully constructed that "nothing — nothing — could possibly go wrong." The author uses this certainty to build dramatic irony: readers sense doom while Haldeman remains blindly confident. The title itself is a double meaning — "cooking the books" means falsifying financial records, but also foreshadows Haldeman's literal, horrifying end in the desert heat.

Vocabulary Dossier: Words From the Scene

Eight key terms identified in Cooking the Books — defined and put to use.

Financial ruin
Noun phrase
A state of complete economic collapse in which a person has no money and cannot pay their debts.
"He was facing financial ruin" — Haldeman had no money left to pay his rent, his ex-wife, or his creditors.
Alibi
Noun
Evidence or a claim that proves a person was somewhere else when a crime was committed, making them innocent.
Haldeman needed Hyatt's phone call to confirm his alibi — proof he was in Palm Springs, not Los Angeles, when the fire started.
Unquestioning
Adjective
Accepting or obeying without asking for reasons or expressing doubt; completely trusting.
Because of Hyatt's unquestioning trust in Haldeman, he never thought to verify the financial data he was entering.
Suspicion
Noun
A feeling or belief that something is wrong or that someone has done something bad, without full proof.
Haldeman falsified the accounts so the insurance company would have no suspicion when they investigated the fire.
Heatwave
Noun
A prolonged period of abnormally hot weather, often dangerous to people's health.
Ricky told Haldeman that August heatwaves in Palm Springs were so severe they killed many elderly residents each year.
Barbecue
Noun / Verb
A method of cooking food over an open flame or hot coals; also the equipment used for this purpose.
Haldeman's restaurant specialized in barbecued food — the very cooking style whose gas pipes he sabotaged to start the fire.
Courtyard
Noun
An open, unroofed space enclosed by the walls of a building or group of buildings.
The Ranchero Motel's rear courtyard — sheltered from desert winds and baking in the sun — became the site of Haldeman's death.
Conscious
Adjective
Awake and aware of one's surroundings; the opposite of unconscious or asleep.
When the telephone rang a second time, Haldeman was "still alive, though not conscious" — he could no longer respond to the calls that might have brought help.
